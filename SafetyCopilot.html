SafetyCopilot

An Interactive AI System for Hazard Analysis of Software Requirements

Danilo Guesela & Jesse Garrison

CSCI 7420 · Human-Computer Interactions

System Design Project Proposal · Human-AI Teaming

Abstract

SafetyCopilot is a completed web-based human–AI decision-support application for the classification and review of software safety requirements. The implemented system combines a React/TypeScript frontend with an ASP.NET Core backend to support project management, requirements-document intake, human classification, AI-assisted classification, comparison, and final engineering review. The human engineer retains responsibility for the final classification.

An exploratory user trial involved three engineers, including one senior engineer experienced in requirements classification and two junior engineers. All three used the application without explanation or training and reported that SafetyCopilot helped them reach final classifications faster than their previous unaided approach. The senior engineer reported that AI explanations and displayed confidence levels helped him consider AI classifications that differed from his own and validate classifications that agreed.

These findings provide initial qualitative evidence of usability, perceived efficiency, and the usefulness of explainable AI. They do not establish statistically significant time savings or classification accuracy improvements. Future work will integrate additional system documentation to provide richer context for analysis and classification.

Introduction

The increasing complexity of software-intensive systems has significantly transformed modern safety engineering. Contemporary systems used in aviation, transportation, healthcare, energy production, defense, and industrial automation often contain millions of lines of software code and interact with humans through sophisticated control architectures. Consequently, failures in requirements engineering can lead directly to hazardous conditions that threaten safety, reliability, and operational performance.

Safety engineering seeks to identify hazards early in the system lifecycle and implement controls that eliminate or reduce risk to acceptable levels. Hazard analysis is therefore a cornerstone of safety assurance activities. According to system safety principles established in MIL-STD-882E [19], safety engineering involves identifying hazards, classifying risks, and implementing mitigations throughout the system lifecycle.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in natural-language understanding, summarization, reasoning support, and document analysis. These capabilities make LLMs potentially valuable tools for analyzing large collections of requirements documents and detecting safety-related concerns. However, current evidence indicates that LLMs exhibit significant variation in performance when applied to hazard analysis and may produce inconsistent recommendations, incorrect hazard classifications, or hallucinated reasoning.

This completed project implements SafetyCopilot as a collaborative assistant for requirements classification, preserving human authority over final safety judgments. An initial trial with three engineers provides qualitative feedback on usability, explanations, confidence indicators, and perceived efficiency.

Research Context

What Human Safety Engineers Do During Hazard Analysis

To understand the significance of SafetyCopilot, it is important to understand how safety engineers currently perform requirements-based hazard analysis in practice.

During early system development, human safety engineers typically:

1. Review System Documentation

Engineers examine system requirements specifications, software requirements documents, Concept of operations documents, architectural descriptions, interface specifications, previous hazard analyses and regulatory requirements.

The objective is to understand system functionality, operating environments, assumptions, and interactions.

2. Identify Potential Hazards

Engineers search for conditions that could lead to injury or loss of life environmental harm property damage mission failure and regulatory violations.

This involves reasoning about failures, incorrect functionality, omitted functionality, and human-system interactions.

3. Evaluate Requirements for Safety Deficiencies

Safety engineers assess whether requirements address known hazards, introduce unsafe system behavior, omit critical safety controls, contain ambiguity, lack verification criteria, create conflicting system behavior.

This activity is particularly labor-intensive because each requirement must be interpreted within the broader operational context.

4. Perform Hazard Analysis Methods

Common techniques include:
  • STPA (Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis)
  • FMEA (Failure Modes and Effects Analysis)
  • Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)
  • Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA)
  • System Requirements Hazard Analysis (SRHA)

STPA has become especially important for software-intensive systems because it can identify hazards arising from interactions and inadequate control rather than component failures alone.

5. Derive Safety Requirements

Once hazards are identified, engineers generate:
  • Safety constraints
  • Safety requirements
  • Risk mitigations
  • Design recommendations

These requirements are integrated into system specifications and traced throughout development.

6. Document Risk Assessments

Safety engineers maintain hazard logs documenting hazard descriptions, causes, severity, probability, risk level, recommended controls and verification strategies.

These requirements are integrated into system specifications and traced throughout development.

Problem Statement

Traditional hazard analysis requires extensive manual review of requirements documents, often resulting in slow analysis cycles, cognitive overload, and inconsistent identification of hazards. Recent research shows that LLMs can assist with hazard identification but may also produce hallucinations or incorrect recommendations, requiring careful human oversight.

The problem this project addresses is whether interactive AI assistance—specifically SafetyCopilot—can improve hazard‑identification accuracy, reduce workload, and support better decision‑making during safety‑engineering tasks.

Literature Review

Software Safety Analysis and Requirements Engineering

Software-intensive systems increasingly concentrate safety-critical behavior in requirements, control logic, interfaces, and interactions among components. Consequently, safety cannot be established solely by testing whether individual components fail. Leveson argues that accidents can emerge from inadequate control of system behavior even when no component has failed, and her Systems-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes (STAMP) reframes safety as a control problem [2]. System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA), which operationalizes this view, begins by identifying losses, hazards, system-level constraints, unsafe control actions, and causal scenarios [3]. This perspective is particularly relevant to SafetyCopilot because a requirement may be syntactically valid and technically feasible while still permitting an unsafe system state. An AI-supported requirements review must therefore reason about context, interactions, missing constraints, and unintended behavior rather than merely search for safety-related keywords.

Requirements documents are a logical intervention point because defects introduced during requirements engineering can propagate into architecture, implementation, verification, and operation. Natural-language requirements are especially susceptible to ambiguity, incompleteness, inconsistency, unverifiability, and the use of vague or subjective expressions. Berry, Kamsties, and Krieger show that ambiguity has multiple linguistic sources and that removing one interpretation does not necessarily make a specification unambiguous [4]. Automated quality-assurance approaches have consequently used rules and natural-language processing (NLP) to identify defects in requirements. Femmer et al., for example, demonstrate that rapid automated checks can detect several quality problems in natural-language requirements and provide feedback during authoring and review [5]. Dalpiaz, Ferrari, Franch, and Palomares similarly map a broad body of NLP research in requirements engineering, while noting persistent challenges involving domain dependence, limited industrial validation, and the semantic complexity of requirements tasks [6].

These studies establish the value of automated screening but also expose an important distinction. Linguistic quality tools commonly detect observable textual patterns, whereas hazard-oriented analysis must infer how a requirement could contribute to harm, what safety constraint is absent, and how the text should be revised. Standards such as ISO 26262 reinforce the need for disciplined hazard analysis, safety requirements, traceability, verification, and lifecycle evidence in safety-related development [7]. SafetyCopilot therefore occupies the intersection of requirements-quality analysis and software safety engineering: it seeks not only to flag potentially unsafe or missing requirements but also to preserve human review and an auditable decision process.

Large Language Models for Safety-Relevant Analysis

Large language models (LLMs) extend earlier NLP approaches because they can perform classification, explanation, revision, and open-ended analysis through natural-language interaction. Their capacity to synthesize information across long documents makes them plausible assistants for knowledge-intensive hazard analysis. The source study cited in the proposal directly examines this possibility: Dokas benchmarks LLMs for hazard analysis in safety-critical systems and frames hallucination as a potential source of safety risk when generated analyses are treated as authoritative [1]. This work is foundational for SafetyCopilot because it supports empirical assessment of LLM-generated hazard analyses while underscoring that fluent output is not equivalent to dependable safety evidence.

The wider literature on hallucination explains why independent human judgment remains necessary. Ji et al. define hallucination as generated content that is nonsensical or unfaithful to the provided source and survey its causes, detection methods, and mitigation strategies [8]. In requirements analysis, an apparently reasonable model response may invent system behavior, overlook a safety constraint, or propose a revision unsupported by the source document. Such failures can create both false positives, which burden analysts and erode trust, and false negatives, which may leave consequential hazards undiscovered. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework accordingly emphasizes validity, reliability, safety, transparency, explainability, accountability, and ongoing risk management across the AI lifecycle [9]. For SafetyCopilot, these principles imply that model outputs should be treated as recommendations with provenance and uncertainty—not as autonomous approvals or replacements for a safety engineer.

The literature also suggests that evaluation should extend beyond aggregate accuracy. Safety analysis involves asymmetric consequences: missing a hazardous requirement is generally more serious than asking an engineer to inspect an additional benign requirement. Precision, recall, false-positive rate, false-negative rate, and class-specific performance are therefore more informative than accuracy alone. Evaluation should additionally test robustness across projects, requirement types, hazard categories, prompt variations, and model versions. Because generative systems may produce different outputs for equivalent inputs, the study should preserve prompts, source requirements, model identifiers, parameters, explanations, proposed revisions, and human decisions. This record is essential for reproducibility and for later examination of whether the AI contribution improved the resulting safety analysis.

Human–AI Collaboration, Trust, and Reliance

SafetyCopilot is best understood as a human–AI decision-support system rather than an autonomous hazard analyst. Research on automation has long shown that introducing a capable tool does not guarantee appropriate use. Parasuraman and Riley distinguish misuse, disuse, and abuse of automation, demonstrating that people may over-rely on a system, reject useful assistance, or deploy automation without adequate regard for its consequences [10]. Lee and See subsequently describe appropriate reliance as a calibration problem: trust should correspond to the automation’s actual capabilities in the current context [11]. In safety engineering, both overtrust and undertrust are problematic. Overtrust may cause an analyst to accept a plausible but incorrect hazard classification, whereas undertrust may eliminate the efficiency and coverage benefits that motivated AI assistance.

Explanations are often proposed as a mechanism for improving trust, but explanation quality must not be conflated with system correctness. Jacovi et al. argue that trustworthiness and user trust must be treated separately and that warranted trust depends on evidence that a system will behave as expected [12]. Liao, Gruen, and Miller further show that explainable-AI needs are question-driven: users may ask what the system predicted, why it did so, how confident it is, what would change the result, or when the system is likely to fail [13]. These distinctions support the proposal’s option to reveal explanations, but they also motivate a stronger experimental question: do explanations improve error detection and decision quality, or do they merely make incorrect recommendations more persuasive?

Empirical work provides reason for caution. Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos show that people can over-rely on AI recommendations and that cognitive-forcing interventions can improve analytical engagement, although such interventions may reduce perceived usability [14]. This tradeoff is directly relevant to SafetyCopilot’s staged workflow. Requiring the engineer to classify requirements before seeing AI output creates an independent judgment and reduces anchoring on the model’s recommendation. The subsequent comparison phase can expose disagreement, after which explanations and revisions can be consulted as decision support. This ordering is methodologically stronger than presenting the AI answer first because it allows the study to distinguish unaided human performance, independent model performance, and changes attributable to interaction.

Interface design can further encourage appropriate reliance. Amershi et al.’s guidelines for human–AI interaction recommend making system capabilities clear, supporting efficient correction, learning from user behavior cautiously, and communicating changes and failures appropriately [15]. Applied to SafetyCopilot, these principles favor requirement-level evidence, visible links to source text, editable recommendations, explicit acceptance or rejection, and preservation of the engineer’s rationale. Confidence indicators should be carefully validated because numerical confidence can itself become an anchoring cue. Where calibrated probabilities are unavailable, the system should communicate uncertainty through evidence coverage, known limitations, model disagreement, or requests for additional context rather than presenting unsupported precision.

Evaluation of SafetyCopilot

The proposal identifies hazard-identification accuracy, false positives, false negatives, completion time, cognitive workload, trust, and reliance as evaluation measures. Together, these variables capture technical effectiveness and human factors, but they should be operationalized separately. Classification performance can be evaluated against a reference set established by qualified safety experts, with disagreements adjudicated and inter-rater agreement reported. Time on task can indicate efficiency, while interaction logs can reveal whether participants inspect explanations, revise answers, accept recommendations, or correct the AI. The NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) offers a well-established multidimensional measure of perceived workload, including mental demand, temporal demand, effort, performance, and frustration [16]. Trust should be measured using a validated scale and interpreted alongside behavioral reliance because reported trust and actual acceptance behavior may diverge.

A controlled evaluation can compare at least three core conditions: human analysis without AI, AI analysis without human intervention, and human–AI collaboration. Within the collaborative condition, explanation availability and uncertainty presentation can be manipulated to test their effects on accuracy, workload, and reliance. The principal outcome should be complementary performance: whether the combined human–AI process identifies hazards or improves requirements more effectively than either agent alone. Error-sensitive analysis is crucial; an intervention that reduces completion time but increases missed hazards would not constitute an improvement for a safety-critical task. Qualitative interviews or think-aloud data can supplement quantitative results by revealing why users accepted, rejected, or modified recommendations.

Synthesis and Research Gap

Existing research offers mature foundations for system-theoretic hazard analysis, automated requirements-quality checking, AI risk management, and calibrated reliance on automation. However, these strands remain insufficiently integrated. Traditional requirements tools tend to focus on detectable linguistic defects; emerging LLM studies demonstrate broader reasoning capabilities but also introduce hallucination and reproducibility risks; and human–AI research shows that explanations can improve understanding while simultaneously encouraging over-reliance. There remains a need for controlled evidence about how safety engineers interact with LLM-generated hazard classifications and requirement revisions, particularly when the human forms an independent judgment before receiving AI assistance.

SafetyCopilot addresses this gap through a human-in-the-loop workflow that separates human and AI classifications, exposes disagreements, permits optional explanations, proposes revisions, and leaves final acceptance with the engineer. Its contribution is therefore not simply another LLM classifier. The proposed system provides an experimental platform for studying whether AI assistance improves hazard detection and requirements quality, how explanations affect warranted trust, and which interaction patterns preserve human accountability. A rigorous study should ultimately demonstrate not only that the model can generate plausible safety recommendations, but that the complete sociotechnical workflow produces safer, more traceable, and more defensible engineering decisions.

System Description

SafetyCopilot is an implemented web application for human–AI teaming in safety-related requirements classification. It supports a staged workflow that preserves the engineer’s independent judgment while making AI recommendations available for review.

  1. Project and document intake: Users organize work into projects and upload requirements documents for extraction and review.
  2. Human classification: Engineers assess and classify requirements.
  3. AI classification: A backend AI classification service produces recommendations and associated explanations and confidence values.
  4. Comparison and analysis: The interface presents human and AI classifications together, including areas of agreement and disagreement.
  5. Final engineer review: Engineers consider the AI evidence and retain authority over the final classification.

AI confidence is a model-provided decision-support indicator, not independent proof of correctness. Explanations likewise require engineering scrutiny.

Human–AI Teaming Features

The user interface supports comparison of human and AI assessments, makes model reasoning visible through explanations and confidence values, and keeps the final decision with the engineer. These design choices support transparency, human oversight, and informed reliance rather than automatic acceptance of AI output.

Methodology and Evaluation

The project combined implementation of a human–AI requirements-review workflow with an initial exploratory user trial. Three engineers used the application without receiving an explanation of its operation. Feedback was collected on ease of use, AI-assisted decision-making, and perceived classification efficiency.

Trial Participants

  • One senior engineer with substantial experience in requirements classification.
  • Two junior engineers.

Evaluation Scope and Limitations

The observations reported here are qualitative feedback from a small, three-person trial. No measured completion-time comparisons, reference-standard accuracy scores, statistical significance tests, or standardized workload scores were supplied. Claims about speed therefore describe participant-reported improvements rather than measured performance. A larger controlled study remains appropriate for quantitative validation.

User Trial and Findings

Three engineers participated; all three were able to use SafetyCopilot without an explanation of how the application worked. The trial included one senior engineer experienced in requirements classification and two junior engineers.

Senior Engineer Feedback

The senior engineer reported that the AI classification's explanation and confidence level helped him more readily accept an AI classification when it differed from his initial judgment. When the AI and human classifications matched, these features helped him validate the shared assessment. This is a report of his experience, not proof that the AI decision was always correct.

Junior Engineer Feedback

Both junior engineers were able to navigate and use the application without any prior explanation, supporting a positive initial assessment of interface learnability.

Perceived Efficiency

All three engineers reported that SafetyCopilot enabled them to complete their final classifications more quickly than when classifying requirements without the application. Exact time savings were not recorded or supplied.

Interpretation

The trial suggests that the interface is approachable for users with different experience levels and that visible AI explanations and confidence can support decision review. Further study is needed to determine whether these benefits translate into measurable gains in accuracy, appropriate reliance, and completion time.

Outcomes and Research Implications

The software implementation is complete for the reported project scope, and the initial user trial has been conducted. Participant feedback indicates that the interface was usable without instruction and that all three participants perceived a faster final classification process.

The senior engineer's feedback specifically supports the relevance of explainable AI to human–AI teaming: explanations and confidence helped him assess both agreement and disagreement with the AI. These observations are promising but preliminary; accuracy, calibrated trust, and objectively measured efficiency remain subjects for further evaluation.

Future Improvements

Integrate Additional System Documentation for Context-Aware Analysis

The principal recommended improvement is to extend SafetyCopilot beyond individual requirements documents by integrating other system documentation into the analysis and classification process. Relevant sources may include system architecture descriptions, interface specifications, concepts of operations, design documents, existing hazard analyses, safety constraints, verification artifacts, and applicable standards.

With appropriate document ingestion, traceability, and context retrieval, the AI could evaluate a requirement against the surrounding system design and operating context, rather than relying mainly on the requirement text alone. The interface should show which supporting documents informed a classification and allow the engineer to inspect them.

Additional Evaluation

Conduct a larger controlled study with recorded task times, expert reference classifications, false-positive and false-negative rates, workload measures, and calibrated trust assessments. This would test whether the favorable trial feedback is reproducible and whether faster decisions preserve or improve classification quality.

Project Conclusion

SafetyCopilot has progressed from a research proposal to a completed application with an ASP.NET Core backend, a React frontend, and an initial three-engineer user trial. The observed ability of all participants to operate the interface without explanation, their reported improvements in classification speed, and the senior engineer's positive experience with AI explanations and confidence provide encouraging early evidence for the human–AI teaming approach.

Future work will focus on integrating broader system documentation and conducting quantitative validation. The central design principle remains unchanged: AI supports engineering judgment, while the human engineer makes the final safety-related classification.
