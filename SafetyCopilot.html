Group Project - Research Proposal: SafetyCopilot

An Interactive AI System for Hazard Analysis of Software Requirements

Abstract

Hazard analysis is a fundamental activity within system safety engineering. It is performed to identify hazards, derive safety constraints, and ensure that system and software requirements adequately mitigate risks before deployment. This analysis is critical, as errors within software logic, programming, and human/software communications have historically resulted in theft, loss of valuable data, property damage, lawsuits, and death [2]. Unfortunately, the process is highly knowledge-intensive, cognitively demanding, and time-consuming, particularly for modern software-intensive and safety-critical systems [1].

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) presents new opportunities for supporting safety engineers by automating portions of requirements analysis and hazard identification. Leveraging LLMs addresses several challenges that project teams tend to face when undergoing Requirements Engineering (RE); these challenges include time and budget constraints [3] At the same time, concerns remain regarding hallucinations, inconsistent reasoning, false hazard detection, and overreliance on AI-generated recommendations.

This research proposes the development and evaluation of SafetyCopilot, an interactive human-AI decision support system designed to assist safety engineers during software requirements hazard analysis. Rather than replacing human expertise, SafetyCopilot is designed as a collaborative tool that allows engineers to independently assess requirements, compare their analyses with AI-generated classifications, review AI recommendations and explanations, and ultimately make final decisions regarding hazard identification and mitigation. Using a controlled experimental design, the study will evaluate whether AI-assisted hazard analysis improves hazard-identification accuracy, reduces task completion time, lowers cognitive workload, and enhances analysis consistency.

Additionally, the research will investigate how explainable AI features influence trust calibration, reliance, and decision quality. The findings will contribute to human-centered AI, explainable AI, safety engineering, and requirements engineering by providing empirical evidence regarding the practical role of LLMs in safety-critical system analysis.

Introduction

The increasing complexity of software-intensive systems has significantly transformed modern safety engineering. Contemporary systems used in aviation, transportation, healthcare, energy production, defense, and industrial automation often contain millions of lines of software code and interact with humans through sophisticated control architectures. Consequently, failures in requirements engineering can lead directly to hazardous conditions that threaten safety, reliability, and operational performance.

Safety engineering seeks to identify hazards early in the system lifecycle and implement controls that eliminate or reduce risk to acceptable levels. Hazard analysis is therefore a cornerstone of safety assurance activities. According to system safety principles established in MIL-STD-882E, safety engineering involves identifying hazards, classifying risks, and implementing mitigations throughout the system lifecycle. [cto.mil], [waru.edu].

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in natural-language understanding, summarization, reasoning support, and document analysis. These capabilities make LLMs potentially valuable tools for analyzing large collections of requirements documents and detecting safety-related concerns. However, current evidence indicates that LLMs exhibit significant variation in performance when applied to hazard analysis and may produce inconsistent recommendations, incorrect hazard classifications, or hallucinated reasoning. [eurekamag.com], [explore.openaire.eu]

This research addresses the need for AI systems that augment, rather than replace, human safety engineers. The proposed SafetyCopilot framework positions AI as a collaborative assistant capable of supporting human decision-making while preserving human authority over safety judgments. The project seeks to determine whether such collaboration improves safety engineering performance and decision quality.

Research Context

What Human Safety Engineers Do During Hazard Analysis

To understand the significance of SafetyCopilot, it is important to understand how safety engineers currently perform requirements-based hazard analysis in practice.

During early system development, human safety engineers typically:

Review System Documentation

Engineers examine:
  • System requirements specifications
  • Software requirements documents
  • Concept of operations documents
  • Architectural descriptions
  • Interface specifications
  • Previous hazard analyses
  • Regulatory requirements

The objective is to understand system functionality, operating environments, assumptions, and interactions.

Identify Potential Hazards

Engineers search for conditions that could lead to:
  • Injury or loss of life
  • Environmental harm
  • Property damage
  • Mission failure
  • Regulatory violations

This involves reasoning about failures, incorrect functionality, omitted functionality, and human-system interactions. [sebokwiki.org], [cto.mil]

Evaluate Requirements for Safety Deficiencies

Safety engineers assess whether requirements:
  • Address known hazards
  • Introduce unsafe system behavior
  • Omit critical safety controls
  • Contain ambiguity
  • Lack verification criteria
  • Create conflicting system behavior

This activity is particularly labor-intensive because each requirement must be interpreted within the broader operational context.

Perform Hazard Analysis Methods

Common techniques include:
  • STPA (Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis)
  • FMEA (Failure Modes and Effects Analysis)
  • Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)
  • Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA)
  • System Requirements Hazard Analysis (SRHA)

STPA has become especially important for software-intensive systems because it can identify hazards arising from interactions and inadequate control rather than component failures alone. [direct.mit.edu], [dspace.mit.edu], [psas.scripts.mit.edu]

Derive Safety Requirements

Once hazards are identified, engineers generate:
  • Safety constraints
  • Safety requirements
  • Risk mitigations
  • Design recommendations

These requirements are integrated into system specifications and traced throughout development. [safetyartisan.com], [dspace.mit.edu]

Document Risk Assessments

Safety engineers maintain hazard logs documenting:
  • Hazard descriptions
  • Causes
  • Severity
  • Probability
  • Risk level
  • Recommended controls
  • Verification strategies

These requirements are integrated into system specifications and traced throughout development. [safetyartisan.com], [dspace.mit.edu]

Problem Statement

Traditional hazard analysis requires extensive manual review of requirements documents, often resulting in slow analysis cycles, cognitive overload, and inconsistent identification of hazards. Recent research shows that LLMs can assist with hazard identification but may also produce hallucinations or incorrect recommendations, requiring careful human oversight.

The problem this project addresses is whether interactive AI assistance—specifically SafetyCopilot—can improve hazard‑identification accuracy, reduce workload, and support better decision‑making during safety‑engineering tasks.

Research Objectives

  • Evaluate whether SafetyCopilot improves hazard‑identification accuracy compared to human‑only analysis.
  • Measure false positives, false negatives, and classification agreement between humans and the LLM.

  • Determine whether AI explanations help users make more informed decisions.

H1: Hazard Identification

Human-AI teams will identify more relevant hazards than human-only participants:

H1 =: P (Human + AI) > P (Human).

H2: False Positives

System Description

SafetyCopilot is a web‑based application that allows users to upload system requirements documents (e.g., PDF files). The system extracts individual requirements and initiates a controlled experimental workflow consisting of five phases:

  1. Human Classification: The human operator labels each requirement as hazard or non‑hazard.
  2. AI Classification: The LLM independently classifies each requirement.
  3. Human vs. AI Comparison: The system displays agreement and disagreement between classifications.
  4. AI Recommendations: The LLM proposes requirement revisions and identifies unsafe or missing requirements.
  5. Human Acceptance: The operator reviews and accepts or rejects AI recommendations.

(All system details sourced from Preproposal.pdf)

Methodology

The research will follow a controlled experimental design using SafetyCopilot as the primary tool for hazard analysis. Participants will analyze a standardized set of software requirements using both human‑only and human‑AI workflows.

Experimental Measures

  • Hazard‑identification accuracy
  • False positives and false negatives
  • Task‑completion time
  • Trust and reliance on AI recommendations

Data Collection

Data will be collected through system logs, participant surveys, and structured observation. All requirement classifications and interactions with SafetyCopilot will be recorded for analysis.

Expected Outcomes

We expect SafetyCopilot to reduce analysis time and cognitive workload while improving consistency in hazard identification. However, we also anticipate that AI explanations will play a critical role in determining whether users trust or question AI‑generated recommendations.

The results will contribute to understanding how LLM‑based tools can be safely integrated into safety‑critical engineering workflows.
